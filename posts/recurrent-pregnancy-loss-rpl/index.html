Recurrent Pregnancy Loss (RPL): Causes, Testing, and How We Can Help

Experiencing multiple miscarriages can be emotionally devastating and physically exhausting. Recurrent Pregnancy Loss (RPL) is a specific clinical condition that requires comprehensive diagnostics rather than just repeated attempts.

At Kindle Womb IVF & Fertility Centre, we provide empathetic care backed by advanced embryology technology to identify root causes and help couples achieve successful, full-term pregnancies.

Defining Recurrent Pregnancy Loss (RPL)

In clinical medicine, Recurrent Pregnancy Loss (RPL) is defined as a sequence of two or more clinical pregnancy losses before the fetus reaches viability (typically defined as 20 to 24 weeks of gestation). Unlike an isolated miscarriage, which is a relatively common event that happens in 10% to 20% of all pregnancies, RPL is a distinct medical condition affecting roughly 1% to 2% of couples. It is important to emphasize that RPL represents a pathology that warrants systematic and comprehensive medical evaluation, rather than simply attributing the losses to random chance or bad luck.

A key distinction in diagnosing RPL is separating clinical pregnancies (confirmed by ultrasound showing a gestational sac or fetal tissue) from biochemical pregnancies (characterized only by transiently elevated hCG levels on blood tests that fade before a sac is visualized). A clinical assessment at our Tonk Road fertility hospital evaluates the precise timing, nature, and pattern of each loss to build a comprehensive history that guides our targeted diagnostic path.

The Primary Causes of Recurrent Miscarriages

The etiology of Recurrent Pregnancy Loss is complex and multi-factorial. The primary causes identified in clinical literature and validated through our patient treatments include:

1. Genetic and Chromosomal Abnormalities
Genetic factors are responsible for up to 50% of early pregnancy losses. In most cases, these are random, numerical chromosomal abnormalities (aneuploidies), such as trisomies, monosomies, or polyploidies, which occur during fertilization or early cell division. The risk of these random errors rises significantly with maternal age, due to the natural aging process of the oocytes.

However, in 2% to 5% of couples experiencing RPL, one of the partners carries a balanced chromosomal translocation or inversion. While the parent is completely healthy and shows no physical symptoms, their germ cells (eggs or sperm) can carry unbalanced genetic material, leading to embryos with missing or duplicated chromosomal segments that are incompatible with life.

2. Anatomical Uterine Factors
The structural integrity and contour of the uterine cavity are essential for stable embryo implantation and fetal development. Congenital uterine anomalies (such as a septate, bicornuate, or unicornuate uterus) can restrict fetal growth or compromise blood supply. Among these, a uterine septum (a band of tissue dividing the cavity) is highly associated with miscarriages because the septum lacks proper blood flow to sustain a developing placenta.

Acquired uterine conditions can also play a major role. These include submucosal uterine fibroids (non-cancerous tumors growing into the cavity), endometrial polyps, and intrauterine adhesions (Asherman's syndrome) caused by past infections or surgical procedures. These growths or scars disrupt the endometrial lining, preventing proper implantation.

3. Immunological and Autoimmune Factors
During a normal pregnancy, the mother's immune system undergoes complex adaptations to tolerate the embryo, which contains foreign paternal DNA. In some cases, abnormal autoimmune responses disrupt this tolerance. The most notable condition is Antiphospholipid Syndrome (APS), an autoimmune disorder where the body produces antibodies that target its own cell membrane proteins. APS promotes blood clot formation in the micro-vessels of the developing placenta, restricting oxygen and nutrients and causing pregnancy failure. Other immunological theories involve imbalances in maternal natural killer (NK) cells or abnormal cytokine profiles, which are actively researched in reproductive medicine.

4. Endocrine (Hormonal) Imbalances
Proper hormonal support is critical to maintaining a pregnancy, particularly during the first trimester. Conditions such as uncontrolled diabetes mellitus, thyroid disorders (hypothyroidism or hyperthyroidism, as well as the presence of thyroid antibodies), and hyperprolactinemia are closely linked to elevated miscarriage risks. Additionally, Luteal Phase Defect (LPD)—where the corpus luteum fails to produce sufficient progesterone to support the early endometrial lining—can prevent the embryo from establishing a secure connection to the maternal blood supply.

5. Hematological Factors (Thrombophilia)
Inherited thrombophilias (genetic blood-clotting disorders such as Factor V Leiden mutations, Prothrombin gene mutations, or Protein C/S deficiencies) can cause micro-thrombi (tiny clots) to form in the placental blood vessels, leading to placental insufficiency and miscarriage, often in the second trimester.

Comprehensive Diagnostic Protocols at Kindle Womb

To break the cycle of recurrent loss, our specialists at Kindle Womb IVF perform systematic, evidence-based diagnostic screenings:

  • Parental Karyotyping: Genetic testing of both partners to check for balanced translocations or structural chromosomal rearrangements.
  • Hysterosalpingography (HSG) and 3D Pelvic Ultrasound: High-definition imaging to map the uterine structure and identify congenital anomalies, fibroids, or polyps.
  • Diagnostic Hysteroscopy: Inserting a thin, lighted telescope (hysteroscope) trans-vaginally to directly inspect the uterine cavity and, if needed, remove scar tissue, polyps, or repair a septum during the same procedure. Learn more on our Fertility Enhancing Surgeries page.
  • Thrombophilia and APS Panels: Specialized blood tests to screen for Lupus Anticoagulant, Anticardiolipin antibodies, and Anti-beta2-glycoprotein I antibodies, along with inherited clotting mutations.
  • Endocrine Screenings: Comprehensive hormone panels evaluating fasting glucose, HbA1c, Thyroid Stimulating Hormone (TSH), prolactin levels, and ovarian reserve markers (AMH).

Advanced Treatment Pathways

Once a specific cause is identified, our clinical team maps out a customized treatment plan:

For anatomical anomalies, surgical correction via hysteroscopy yields excellent outcomes. Repairing a uterine septum or removing submucosal fibroids restores the natural environment of the womb, allowing for normal implantation.

For blood clotting disorders (like APS) or inherited thrombophilias, we prescribe low-dose aspirin combined with low-molecular-weight heparin (LMWH) injections, starting from early pregnancy. This protocol prevents micro-clots from forming in the developing placenta, significantly increasing live birth rates.

For chromosomal anomalies, the most successful approach is In Vitro Fertilization (IVF) combined with Preimplantation Genetic Testing for Aneuploidies (PGT-A). By growing embryos to the blastocyst stage (Day 5) and testing a few cells from the outer layer (trophectoderm), our embryologists can identify and transfer only chromosomally normal (euploid) embryos, bypassing the primary driver of genetic miscarriages.
